# 12 · Behavioural Round, Questions to Ask, Logistics 🔴
**Time needed: 45 minutes. Say every answer out loud.**

---

## First, the context: why this round is heavier than you expect

UK banks typically run the behavioural round as a **structured competency interview** —
prepared questions, asked the same way to every candidate, scored against a framework. That
is different from the informal "tell me about yourself" chat common in Indian services
interviews.

**What that means practically:**

- **Prepared examples matter.** "I would generally…" scores badly. "On Tradu, we had…"
  scores well. They are listening for a specific situation, not a philosophy.
- **Every answer needs a result.** A story that ends at what you did, without what happened,
  reads as incomplete to someone scoring against a rubric.
- **You will get follow-ups on the same story** — "what would you do differently", "how did
  the other person react". Pick examples you remember properly, not the most impressive one.

**Use STAR:** Situation → Task → Action → Result. Under 90 seconds each.

> ⚠️ I cannot confirm Lloyds' exact framework or round structure. Ask your recruiter what
> the behavioural round covers — it is a normal question and they usually tell you.

## The seven questions to prepare

Have a **specific, real** example for each. Two of them can share a story; the rest should
not.

### 1. 🔴 "Tell me about a technical problem you solved."
Use a defect you root-caused. Land on **what changed afterwards** — a test, a log line, a
check — so it becomes a quality story rather than a firefighting one.

### 2. 🔴 "Tell me about a time you disagreed with someone."
The pattern that scores: you raised the concern, then brought **evidence or an alternative**
rather than just objecting, and you accepted the outcome either way.

> "In a review I flagged that [X]. Rather than just saying it was wrong, I [showed the case
> where it broke / wrote a quick test demonstrating it]. We [outcome]. What I have learned is
> that showing beats arguing — it turns an opinion into something we can both look at."

### 3. 🔴 "Tell me about a mistake you made."
**Have one. Refusing to answer looks far worse than the mistake.** Real, owned plainly,
ending in what you changed.

A missed edge case caught in QA, with "I added a test for that case and started checking the
negative paths in review", is a good shape. Do not pick something that suggests carelessness
with money or data.

### 4. "How do you handle pressure or a tight deadline?"
Scope, communicate early, never silently slip. You worked in sprints with QA — you know what
happens when something half-finished reaches testing.

### 5. "Tell me about working with someone difficult, or in a team."
Your frontend collaboration is good material: two people with different priorities agreeing
an API contract. The useful point is **agreeing the contract early** rather than discovering
the mismatch in integration.

### 6. "How do you learn something new?"
> "Documentation first, because tutorials teach you one path and the docs teach you the
> model. Then I build something small, because reading does not surface the problems. Then I
> look at how it is already used in the codebase, since the team's conventions matter more
> than my preferences."

### 7. 🔴 "Why do you want to work at Lloyds?"
Do not say "it is a big company" or "for growth".

> "Two reasons that are specific to me. First, the domain — I have spent nearly two years on
> a trading platform doing onboarding, identity verification and payments across multiple
> vendors, so financial services is where my experience actually is, and I would rather build
> on that than start again somewhere unrelated.
>
> Second, I want to work on a product that is built and run over the long term rather than a
> client project I hand over. That changes how you write things — you care about tests,
> logging and maintainability differently when you are the one who will be supporting it in
> two years. The quality bar being high in a regulated environment is a reason I want it, not
> a reason to be nervous about it."

That answer is strong because it is true and because almost nobody says the second part.

## Two questions to expect about your situation

**"Why are you leaving / why did the role end?"** → See file 11, section 5.3. Short,
factual, no criticism of the employer. Frame as moving **toward** something.

**"You have under two years. Why are you ready for this?"**
> "I would not claim to be a senior engineer. What I would say is that the two years were
> dense — I worked across the full cycle rather than just writing code: tests, defect
> analysis, refactoring, code review and releases, on a platform handling real money. I am
> looking for somewhere I can go deeper with people who are further along, and I would rather
> be the one asking questions in a strong team than the most experienced person in a weak
> one."

## 🔴 Questions YOU ask them

Have four ready, ask two or three. Ending with "no, I'm good" reads as no interest.

**Technical:**
1. "What does the service I would be working on actually do, and who uses it?"
2. "What does the stack look like day to day — Spring Boot version, cloud platform, how you
   deploy?"
3. "How much of the work is new build versus modernising existing systems?"
4. "What does the testing and review process look like before something reaches production?"

**Team and growth:**
5. "What would a successful first three months look like for someone joining at my level?"
6. "Who would I be learning from, and how does code review work in the team?"
7. "How does the team handle production support — is there an on-call rota?"

**The one that makes you memorable:**
> "What is the biggest technical problem the team is working on right now?"

Then **listen**, and connect your answer to it. That is how you close an interview.

## Logistics

| Question | How to answer |
|---|---|
| **Notice period** | You are not currently employed, so **you are immediately available**. Say it clearly — it is a genuine advantage over candidates serving 60 or 90 days. |
| **Location / relocation** | Know whether the role is Hyderabad-based and whether you can be there. Answer directly; do not hedge. |
| **Work from office** | Most banks are largely back in office. Answer honestly. |
| **Shift overlap** | A UK bank means some overlap with UK hours. You worked with FXCM, so say that — you know what it involves. |
| **Salary** | Know your last fixed CTC and your target **before** the call. Never inflate your previous CTC; it is verified against payslips. Ask for the fixed/variable split at offer stage. |
| **Other offers** | Honest and brief. Do not invent leverage. |

## On the day

- Printed resume, ID, laptop charged, charger, backup hotspot, water.
- **Know your own resume cold.** Everything on it is fair game — Spring Security, Hibernate,
  JUnit, Mockito, SonarQube, Jenkins, Docker, AWS, Kafka, React, **and the AI tools line**.
  If you touched something briefly, say so rather than overstating.
- If online: join five minutes early, test camera and microphone, plain quiet background.
- Smart dress. Slightly overdressed beats underdressed.

## If you get stuck

- **Do not freeze — narrate.** "Let me think about that for a second" is a complete sentence.
- **Do not bluff.** "I have not used that directly. My understanding is X — is that the
  direction you mean?" has never cost anyone an offer. Bluffing has.
- **Ask what they are getting at.** "Do you mean at the database level or in the application?"
- If you fumble one question, let it go. People lose interviews by replaying question 4 while
  answering question 7.
